Sand dunes are among the most restless landforms on the surface of the earth. They form wherever a
supply of loose grains, a wind strong enough to move them, and a place for the grains to settle all
occur together. None of these three conditions is unusual on its own. What is unusual is the way they
combine to produce shapes that repeat themselves across enormous distances with a regularity that
looks almost deliberate. A traveller crossing a great sand sea will pass ridge after ridge of nearly
the same height, spaced at nearly the same interval, curving in nearly the same direction. The
pattern is not designed. It emerges from the simple physics of grains bouncing along the ground.

The movement of sand by wind begins with a process known as saltation. When the wind blows over a bed
of dry grains, it exerts a drag on the topmost layer. Below a certain speed nothing happens, because
the weight of each grain and the friction between neighbouring grains hold the bed together. Above
that threshold the most exposed grains begin to roll, and a few of them lift briefly into the moving
air. Once a grain is airborne it is carried forward and downward in a long flat arc. It strikes the
bed again with far more energy than the wind alone could have given it, and that impact throws other
grains into the air. Within a short distance the number of grains in flight grows rapidly until the
moving cloud of sand itself slows the wind near the ground, and the whole system settles into a
balance.

This balance explains a great deal about the shapes that follow. Because the grains travel in low
hops rather than in suspension, most of the sand stays within a knee height of the ground even in a
strong gale. Fine dust behaves differently. Particles smaller than a certain size are lifted high and
carried away for hundreds of leagues, which is why the air above a desert can be hazy while the sand
itself barely leaves the ground. The sorting is remarkably sharp. A handful of sand taken from the
crest of a dune will contain grains of a narrow range of sizes, while the material at the foot of a
nearby rock slope may contain everything from silt to pebbles.

Once sand is in motion, any obstacle that slows the wind will cause deposition. A tuft of grass, a
low stone, even a slight hollow in the ground can be enough. Sand gathers in the sheltered pocket
behind the obstacle, and the growing mound becomes an obstacle in its own right, which encourages
further gathering. This is the essential feedback that turns a scatter of grains into a landform. The
mound grows until its windward face has a gentle slope, up which grains are driven, and its leeward
face becomes steep, because grains falling over the crest pile up until the slope can hold no more.

The angle at which a pile of dry grains finally gives way is termed the angle of repose. For most
desert sand it lies a little above thirty degrees from the horizontal. When the leeward face reaches
that angle, further deposition triggers a small avalanche, and a thin tongue of sand slides down to
the base. These avalanches are the mechanism by which a dune moves. Grains are lifted up the shallow
windward slope, tipped over the crest, and buried at the foot of the steep face, where they remain
until the whole dune has passed over them and they are exposed on the windward side once more. In
this way the dune travels forward while the individual grains within it merely circulate.

The rate of travel depends chiefly on size. A small dune has less sand to move and therefore advances
quickly. A very large one may creep forward only a few paces in a year. Because small dunes overtake
large ones, and because a small dune that runs into a large one is usually absorbed, the size
distribution in a mature sand sea tends toward larger and fewer forms over long periods. This is one
of several ways in which dune fields behave like a slow fluid, with mergers, collisions and even
something resembling waves passing through them.

Not all dunes have the same shape, and the differences are informative. Where sand is scarce and the
wind blows steadily from one direction, isolated crescent shaped dunes appear, with two horns
pointing downwind. The horns form because the thinner edges of the dune move faster than the thick
middle. Where sand is plentiful under the same steady wind, the crescents join at their horns into
long transverse ridges lying across the wind. Where the wind blows from two directions that differ by
a wide angle, the ridges align with the resultant of the two, producing long parallel ridges that can
run for many leagues almost without a break. Where winds arrive from several directions in turn, the
result is a star shaped mound with arms radiating from a central peak, and such a dune tends to grow
upward rather than travel.

The largest forms of all are sometimes termed draa, and they carry smaller dunes on their flanks in
the way that ocean swells carry ripples. The hierarchy is not accidental. Each scale of form responds
to a different scale of the wind, and each takes a different length of time to adjust. A ripple on
the surface of a dune reorganises itself within minutes of a change in wind direction. The dune
beneath it takes years. The great ridge beneath the dune may preserve the memory of a wind pattern
that has not existed for many centuries, which is why some of these features are described as relict.

Ripples deserve a paragraph of their own, because they are produced by a subtly different mechanism.
They are not miniature dunes. A dune grows where the wind slows, but a ripple grows because of the
geometry of the impacts made by saltating grains. A grain arriving at a shallow angle is more likely
to strike the upwind side of a small bump than the sheltered downwind side, so the upwind side loses
grains faster and the crest migrates. The spacing of ripples turns out to be related to the typical
length of a saltation hop, which in turn depends on wind speed and grain size. On a coarse bed the
ripples are widely spaced and their crests are lined with the largest grains, which are too heavy to
be thrown far and so accumulate where they are pushed.

Water plays a larger part in desert landscapes than the popular picture suggests. Rain is rare, but
when it falls it often falls hard, and with little vegetation to slow it the runoff is immediate. Dry
channels that have held nothing for years can fill within an hour, carry a great load of sediment for
a day, and be dry again by the following week. Much of the loose material that the wind later
redistributes was originally delivered by such floods and left in broad fans where the channels
emerged from the hills. In this sense the wind is a sorting and rearranging force working on a supply
that water provided.

Moisture also affects the sand directly. Even a trace of water between grains creates a thin bridge
that holds them together and raises the wind speed needed to set them moving. A damp surface can be
almost immobile in a wind that would strip a dry one bare. Dew, morning fog and shallow ground water
therefore act as brakes on dune movement, and their seasonal rhythm can produce a corresponding
rhythm in the rate of advance. Where the water table lies close enough to the surface, dunes may
stall entirely and become fixed, at which point plants can take hold and lock them in place for
centuries.

Vegetation is the other great stabiliser. A plant slows the wind near the ground, traps grains among
its stems and roots, and grows upward as sand accumulates around it. The result is a mound anchored
by the plant, which behaves quite differently from a free dune. If the plant dies, whether through
drought, grazing or fire, the mound can be breached and a bowl shaped hollow opens on the windward
side. Sand blows out of the hollow and piles into a long arm downwind. Such hollows and arms are common on
coasts and can reactivate a whole dune field that had appeared settled for generations.

Coastal dunes differ from desert ones in their supply rather than their physics. The beach delivers a
continuous ribbon of fresh sand, replenished by waves and currents, and the wind carries it inland
whenever the surface dries between tides. Because the supply is concentrated along a line, coastal
dunes usually form a belt parallel to the shore, with the youngest and lowest ridge nearest the water
and progressively older ridges behind it. Counting the ridges and dating the soils that have formed
on them gives a record of how the shoreline has shifted, and such records have proved valuable for
understanding changes in sea level over the past several thousand years.

Dunes are also found where no desert and no coast exists. Beyond the margins of former ice sheets,
strong winds blowing off the ice worked on the bare ground left behind by melting, building ridges
that are now buried under forest and farmland. They can be recognised from the air by their gentle
curved outlines, and from the ground by the sandy soil that overlies heavier material. Elsewhere,
along the banks of large rivers, sand exposed at low water is picked up and heaped into small fields
of dunes that vanish again during the next flood.

The study of all these forms has practical worth. Roads, canals and settlements built at the edge of
a sand sea must contend with dunes that continue to advance regardless of what is placed in their
way. The remedies fall into a few families. One may fix the sand with plantings, or with a surface
coating, or with a grid of low fences that break the wind. One may divert the sand by shaping the
ground so that the wind accelerates and carries the grains past the vulnerable place. One may simply
remove the sand as it arrives, which is expensive but sometimes the only option where a dune is very
large. Choosing between these depends on knowing how much sand passes a given line each year, and
that amount can be estimated from wind records with reasonable confidence.

Finally, dunes are excellent recorders. The layers preserved inside an ancient dune, now turned to
stone, tilt at the angle of the leeward face that produced them, and the direction of that tilt
reveals the direction of a wind that blew long before any living thing walked the earth. Whole
mountains of such stone stand in several parts of the world, their cross bedded layers curving and
truncating one another in a pattern that any observer of a modern dune field would recognise at once.
The grains are the same, the slopes are the same, and the rules that arranged them have not changed.
That continuity is perhaps the most satisfying thing about the subject. A person who understands why
a small ridge of sand behaves as it does on a windy afternoon has, in a real sense, understood a
process that has been shaping landscapes for a very long time and will go on shaping them long after
the present arrangement of ridges has been swept into some new and equally regular pattern.
